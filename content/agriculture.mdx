## Begin with the buyer’s calendar

Agricultural marketing works best when it respects the timing of real decisions. Equipment research, input purchasing, distribution planning, and post-harvest reviews happen at different moments. Those moments also vary by crop, region, and business model.

Map the buying calendar with your commercial team. Record when customers begin researching, when they compare options, and when they commit. Do not assume that a calendar built for one market transfers directly to another.

## Give each phase a useful role

Before a purchasing window, publish educational content that explains the questions buyers should ask. During evaluation, make product information and practical comparisons easy to find. After purchase, share onboarding, maintenance, or application resources reviewed by the appropriate experts.

For a supplier, this might mean an early-season checklist, a product demonstration during the evaluation period, and a support-focused series once equipment is in use. The content should reflect actual availability and service coverage.

## Create a flexible production plan

Prepare evergreen explanations and photography in advance. Leave space for changes in local conditions, stock, and customer priorities. A rigid schedule can result in a campaign that arrives after the relevant decision has already been made.

Capture useful footage when your team is already in the field. Obtain permission for identifiable people and locations, and avoid presenting one demonstration as proof of outcomes across every condition.

## Review by season and segment

Look at enquiries by customer type and campaign period. Ask whether the content reached the right buyers while they still had time to act.

Use that review to adjust the next cycle. The goal is a repeatable publishing rhythm that follows the customer’s world, with enough flexibility for the realities of agriculture.
